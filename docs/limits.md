# Operating limits

- **Persistence.** Sleep and checkpoint save the container’s root filesystem. Mounted volumes, including scratch `/tmp`, are excluded. If GKE evicts a pod, the last completed snapshot is the recovery point. Keep separate backups for history.
- **Wake time and snapshot size.** Both grow with the number of files, more than with their bytes. GKE recreates every file before the sandbox starts. In Energy’s production cluster a sandbox with about 25,000 files wakes in about 6.5 s, and ones with about 200,000 files took 30 to 45 s. In a test, 200,000 small files totaling 98 MB produced a 3 GB snapshot. Keep package caches and other disposable files on `/tmp`.
- **Disk.** A sandbox’s root filesystem has the size given as `disk`, 20 GiB by default. `df` reports it, and a write past it fails with “No space left on device” while the sandbox keeps running. Scratch `/tmp` is separate and has no size of its own. Kubernetes counts the root filesystem, `/tmp`, and logs against the `ephemeral-storage` limit, 40 GiB by default, and evicts a sandbox that passes it, so the difference between the two is what a full sandbox can still hold in `/tmp`. `disk` must be below that limit. A sleeping sandbox does not wake into a `disk` smaller than the files it holds, so do not lower it below what sandboxes already use. On GKE 1.36.4 a sandbox with a 1 GiB disk refused the write at 1 GiB, kept running with 900 MiB more in `/tmp`, and woke from its snapshot with the same size.
- **Memory.** The watchdog acts when use comes within an eighth of the sandbox’s limit, and at least 256 MiB below it. It skips processes under 64 MiB and cannot free files kept in `/dev/shm`. It guards one sandbox’s limit. If a whole node runs out of memory, Kubernetes still evicts sandboxes, starting with those furthest over their request. In gVisor sandboxes limited to 1 GiB and 4 GiB it ended a program allocating about 1.5 GiB a second every time, in 24 runs, and the sandbox was killed without it. It adds about 3 millicores to an idle 4 GiB sandbox under bash and about 10 under other shells. A smaller limit leaves less room, so it looks more often and costs more.
- **Capacity.** By default a sandbox requests 0.25 vCPU, 1 GiB of memory, and 4 GiB of temporary storage. Its limits are 2 vCPU, 4 GiB, and 40 GiB respectively. Change them with `resources`. A low-priority placeholder pod keeps capacity available for new sandboxes. Size the pool for your peak traffic and regional quota.
- **Access.** Keep the client in a trusted backend. It can execute commands and read files across its namespace. Your app owns user authorization and billing. The network rules allow DNS and outgoing public internet traffic and block incoming pod traffic. With `directCommands`, you admit your backend to one port, where each sandbox accepts only its own token over an unencrypted connection. Keep the workload identity metadata endpoint blocked. Snapshots are read and written by GKE’s own service agent, so a sandbox’s identity has no access to the snapshot bucket.
- **Identity.** Use a DNS-label sandbox ID of up to 56 characters. To keep custom labels across sleep, include them in the snapshot policy’s grouping rules. Use separate manager selectors or namespaces for independent deployments. The `onOperation` and `onPodLost` callbacks report timings and failures.
